12two halvesthen

Legal training has two halves: knowing the law and being able to do it. Both halves start at the university, and continue at work. At university, knowledge typically comes first and capability follows later in studies. At work the balance flips. Capability drives the work, and knowledge keeps accumulating on the side.

In both places capability is made the same way. Because I am a recovering crossfitter with bad knees, allow me to use a metaphor from the box. At the box, we used to say that to see some progress you need to lift heavy.

Ok. What happens with AI? At work, the machines are now taking that lifting. Nobody has good data on how fast, but the pressure is plain: nobody pays a junior to do in hours what a machine does in a minute.

At the university, it may be worse. Traditionally, the lifting of capability building is done in the shadows. Students read and think at home, libraries, on park benches, wherever. What forced the heavy lifting to be done was the exams, essays, the seminar papers and the theses. Now, much of the capability-building endproducts may be gone.

We all remember being twenty. So we have to presume that essays, seminar papers and theses written at home no longer tell us who learned what.

So the machines are taking the heavy lifting in both places.

And it gets worse. Because the machines will argue well. They will argue hard. They will argue in the firms and in the courts, and they will argue at three in the morning when everybody is asleep. And they will argue just as well when they are wrong. That will be the hard part.

14the gymthen

We all know how a gym works. Well, ideally at least. You buy a membership. And then you actually go there. You don't skip leg day. You don't do instagram. You lift. Systematically. One day at a time. At first, progress is easy to see. Later, you have to work to even stay where you are.

In a legal gym, people read, write and argue in a room with other people. Themselves. With trainers, that is us, giving feedback.

But there's a difference to real gyms. The gym is not enough. You need more than your sessions with the personal trainer around. And that's where the machines come into the picture. They can be useful tools in allowing people to put in the extra hours. They can be the trainer that never gets tired and never gets frustrated.

But, I want to stress this, it cannot be just AI, and somebody has to make sure that the AI is a useful trainer.

15four things to buildthen

None of this will be easy, and none of it will be cheap. It will be a multi-year build, and it will take both infrastructure and people.

The first thing to build is the space and time, both physical and intellectual. We need concrete rooms and hours where the students do the work themselves, and those hours have to count towards the degree.

The second is human feedback. At work, a partner read the draft and sent it back. We need teachers and fellow students who do the same, often and closely, perhaps in less toxic ways than the bosses of yore.

The third is AI buddies for the extra hours. While reading and thinking have to come first, and the student has to be able to do both without AI, machines do have a role to play. They can ask questions and debug cognitive processes. But those things have to be purpose-built.

The fourth thing is proof. Proof of work, not proof of stake. Right now we are losing it. With takehome exams, essays, papers, theses we are losing a view into whether our students can do law. So we need ways to see them do it, in the room and without the machine. Without that proof, we cannot tell whether anything we build is working.

And then there is the question of when to start.
